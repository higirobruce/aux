01 · Premise

Why a mix-only DAW

Every modern DAW is two products glued together: an arrangement workstation (timeline, MIDI, instruments, takes, comping) and a mixing console. The mixing side is almost always the worse half — buried under tracking workflows, cluttered by features the mixer never uses, and tied to a desktop install.

A growing class of users never tracks in a DAW. They get stems from clients, collaborators, or AI tools, and their job starts at the mix bus. For them, 90% of Pro Tools is dead weight, and 50% of Studio One is too.

Thesis

If we drop arrangement entirely, every pixel and every shortcut can serve the mix. The product becomes shallower in features but deeper where it counts — gain staging, routing, dynamics, loudness, delivery.

02 · Principles

Design principles

01

The mix is the home screen

Open a session, the mixer is on. No project tree to navigate, no library to expand. The first surface is the work.

02

Loudness is a first-class citizen

LUFS / TP / LRA are visible at all times. Platform targets (Spotify −14, Apple −16, YouTube −14, broadcast −23) are presets, not math homework.

03

Reference tracks are infrastructure

A/B against commercial masters is one key away. Loudness-matched by default so you compare tone, not volume.

04

Versions are git, not "save as"

Every meaningful change snapshots. Branch a mix, A/B the snapshots, send a link to the client.

05

The session lives where you choose

Stems and project state — in our cloud or on your machine. Privacy-first local sessions. Cloud sessions for share-link delivery and any-device access. BYO bucket lands in v2. The engineer picks per project.

06

Smart defaults, not auto-mix

aux suggests gain staging, group balance, EQ moves — but it never commits. The engineer accepts, edits, or ignores.

How matches happen

Three signals, weighted: name similarity (string edit distance with track-role aware heuristics — "kick", "kk", "BD" all alias), length proximity (within 100 ms), and a spectral fingerprint (FFT-based, like Chromaprint — 30-band time-frequency profile compared by cosine similarity). Above 85% is auto-matched; 60–85% asks the engineer to confirm; below 60% surfaces as unmatched.

What the engineer keeps

The session document is parameter-addressed (§16.02), so swapping a stem replaces only the src reference on its clips. Every other atom — fader position, plugin chain, EQ band gains, send levels, automation lanes, group bus routing — is untouched. The mix moved with the engineer, not with the stem.

No API key

Fingerprinting runs in a Web Worker using the same WASM FFT we use for the spectrum analyzer. String distance is JS. No server inference, no AI API, no audio leaves the machine — even in Local storage mode (§16.06).

What we borrow from Ozone

The interactive curve over a live spectrum; reference-spectrum overlay; numbered drag nodes; dynamic-EQ as a per-band toggle; a "Match" workflow that proposes moves against a target. The visual language of a mastering plugin that gives the engineer x-ray vision into the mix.

What we borrow from Studio One

One brand accent (azure) instead of Ozone's rainbow; serif headings paired with mono values; dark surface with restraint — no chrome, no bevels, no faux skeuomorphism. The kind of plugin window you can stare at for eight hours without eye fatigue.

Decision · 04

The Smart panel is the embodiment of "helpful, never automatic." It proposes, explains why, and waits. Accept applies the move; Discard removes the suggestion; nothing changes until the engineer clicks. This is the line we said we wouldn't cross — and the surface that holds it.

Reading the curve

The dashed gold diagonal is unity — what comes in, goes out. The white curve peels off it at the threshold, bending toward a 4:1 slope. The amber band is the knee — the curve bends through it rather than at a hard corner. The green dot is live input: where it sits on the curve tells you, in one glance, how much gain reduction you're applying right now.

Why a single picture works

Three values that engineers normally hold in their head — threshold, ratio, knee — live on the page as a single shape. Change a knob, the curve moves; you don't have to translate. The GR meter is the consequence: same physics, expressed as how much the loud parts are being held back at this moment.

Decision · 04b

Every dynamics plugin in the suite — bus comp, channel comp, de-esser, limiter, multiband — uses this same chassis: transfer curve, GR meter, knob row. Same place for the same controls, same surface, same accent. Learn one, learn all five.

Why this exists

"If it sounds good everywhere, it sounds good" is the oldest principle in mixing. Engineers who can afford it pay $200/yr for Sonarworks or Realphones to get something like this. We make it native because we already own the master output bus — convolution + EQ in the worklet, no extra DSP cost, no extra wallet.

The Smart layer

The cross-room linter is a spectral diff: render the master through each room's IR offline, compare to the studio reference, surface the deltas. The suggestion above the linter is the targeted move that improves the worst rooms without breaking the best — a constraint solver across eight curves, not a single-room EQ match.

No API key

Pure browser-side DSP — FIR convolution + linear filtering in the AudioWorklet, post-master. Cross-room linter is spectral heuristics per §16.04 v1. No server inference, no AI API. The 8 room impulse responses ship with the app (~3 MB total).

What's measured

ITU BS.1770-4 for LUFS (integrated / short / momentary). 4× oversampled true-peak detection. Cross-correlation between L/R for phase / polarity. L+R sum vs. independent for mono compatibility. Band-limited RMS in 5–9 kHz for sibilance. Plugin-reported latency vs. graph topology. Bus reachability graph traversal for sanity. All standard, all client-side, all in a Web Worker — runs in < 2 seconds for a typical 3-min session.

Why "warnings can override"

Engineers know things the linter doesn't. Sometimes you want the BGV phase-narrow on mono — it's the effect. The pre-flight gates fails (real, costly mistakes) but lets the engineer override warnings with one click. Per §18.04: helpful, never automatic. The check shows up; the move belongs to the engineer.

No API key

Every check is standard audio analysis math — LUFS per ITU BS.1770, true-peak via oversampled interpolation, phase via cross-correlation, mono via L+R sum/diff energy, sibilance via band-limited RMS. Pure client-side DSP in a Web Worker. No server inference, no AI API.

15 · Tech

Browser-as-DAW: what's actually possible

The audio engine

The browser has finally caught up. The viable stack:

  • AudioWorklet for the realtime graph (low-latency, off-main-thread)
  • WASM for DSP — EQ, comp, limiter ports of well-known algos compile cleanly
  • SharedArrayBuffer for the audio ring buffer between worker and worklet
  • OffscreenCanvas + WebGL for waveform / spectrum rendering at 60fps
  • OPFS (Origin Private File System) to cache stems locally — no re-download per session
  • WebCodecs for MP3/AAC encoding on render; FLAC/WAV via JS

Latency budget

Web Audio in a worklet can hit ~10ms round-trip on a modern Mac with a decent interface. That's tracking territory. For mixing — where you're not monitoring through the DAW in realtime — there is no latency problem.

Storage & sync

Stems are immutable blobs (S3). The session document is a parameter-addressed JSON tree — single-author in v1, atom paths designed so a Yjs CRDT layers cleanly on top when real-time collab arrives in v2. Plugin state is just JSON.

Hard

Third-party plugin support is the existential question. VST/AU don't run in the browser. Options: (a) ship a desktop "bridge" that exposes local VST/AU to the web app over WebRTC; (b) cultivate a WASM-native plugin ecosystem; (c) accept it and compete on native quality. v1 should pick (c).

16 · Architecture

Seven commitments before we write code

The product decisions in §18 say what to build. These seven say how. They're load-bearing: each one shapes everything that depends on it, and getting any of them wrong is the kind of mistake that ends in a rewrite. We lock them now.

01 · Audio engine

One AudioWorklet. Internal scheduler. Hot-swap graph via atomic pointer swap. 128-sample blocks at 48 kHz. Sample-accurate parameter ramps everywhere.

The audio thread lives in a single worklet — DSP doesn't fight itself across multiple thread contexts. An internal scheduler walks the node graph each block, skips muted or inactive nodes, and budgets CPU per call. When the user adds a plugin or reroutes a send, we build the new graph on a worker, shadow-render two blocks against it to validate, then atomically swap a pointer at a block boundary on the audio thread. No clicks, no fade-outs, no glitches.

128 samples ≈ 2.67 ms at 48 kHz — short enough for tracking-grade latency, long enough to amortize per-block setup. Every parameter change ramps sample-accurately over a default 10 ms; every knob in the design system implies a ramped value, not a step. 

// graph hot-swap
main      →  session edit  →  serialize change
worker    →  build new graph  →  shadow-render 2 blocks  →  ready
audio     →  atomic pointer swap at next block boundary
              process() reads new graph on the next call

Not chosen: one worklet per node (cross-thread sync nightmare). Pause-rebuild-resume (audible gap on every edit).

Every plugin's process() receives pre-smoothed parameter arrays of length nFrames, never single values. The plugin API enforces this.

02 · Session document

Plain JSON with parameter-level atoms. Autosave to disk (Local) or API (Cloud). Snapshots are named, ordered, diffable commits.

JSON because it's debuggable, git-diffable, exportable, and needs no migration tooling. v1 ships solo-mixer only (collab is v2+), so we skip CRDT machinery entirely — the doc is single-author, autosaved every few seconds to the active storage backend. Snapshots are full document captures with a parent pointer, forming a commit graph the engineer can name, diff, and revert to.

Atoms are per parameter: every knob, every fader, every EQ band gain, every clip start, every plugin parameter is independently addressable. That granularity is the precondition for Pro-Tools-grade automation, sample-accurate undo, and meaningful diffs between mixes. When real-time collab returns in v2, the same atom model layers cleanly under a CRDT (likely Yjs) without rewriting the doc.

03 · Native plugin API

Declarative parameter schema. WASM DSP with a four-function contract. GUI as a constrained component tree — the host renders, the plugin never touches DOM.

We have ~12 plugins to ship in the native suite. If we write the EQ as a one-off, the compressor will diverge, and by the limiter we'll have twelve different code shapes. The API exists so all twelve are the same thing with different DSP inside.

Parameters are declarative. The host knows how to render a knob for any parameter from its schema — no plugin-side rendering. Skew, units, modulation, automation all fall out of the type. 

type Param = {
  id: string;
  label: string;
  unit: 'dB' | 'Hz' | 'ms' | 'st' | '%' | { text: string };
  type: 'continuous' | 'discrete' | 'boolean';
  range: { min: number; max: number; default: number; step?: number };
  skew?: 'linear' | 'log' | 'exp' | number;  // curve factor
  modulatable: boolean;
  automatable: boolean;
};

DSP is a WASM module exporting four functions. Everything else — UI, automation, presets, undo — is the host's problem. 

init(sampleRate: f32, maxBlockSize: u32) -> ptr
process(input: ptr, output: ptr, params: ptr, nFrames: u32)
setState(json: ptr, len: u32)
getState() -> ptr

GUI is a tree, not DOM. The plugin returns a layout description; the host renders. Plugins can't escape sandbox, can't track users, and get re-skinned for free when the design system changes.

Not chosen: letting plugins render their own UI (security + consistency disaster). One-off APIs per plugin (12 forks of the same problem).

When v2 opens this API to third-party WASM plugins, the same contract applies. No carve-outs for native; no second-class for extensions.

04 · Smart engine

Phased: v1 heuristics, v2 client-side WASM ML, v3 opt-in server inference. The suggestion-card surface stays stable across all three.

The Smart EQ panel we already built is the contract — a card with badge, frequency, amount, reason, accept/discard. What's behind it changes; the user never sees a difference.

v1 — heuristics. Rule-based DSP analysis: resonance detection (peak-to-RMS ratio with a moving spectral floor), masking analysis (overlapping bus energy in critical bands), spectral imbalance vs. a target curve, LUFS gap analysis, transient density estimation. Transparent, fast, debuggable, deterministic. Ships day one.

v2 — local ML. Small ONNX models running in a Web Worker for genre classification, instrument detection, masking suggestions. Client-side, no API cost, no audio leaves the machine.

v3 — opt-in server. Heavy models for high-end reference matching. Anonymized feature vectors sent to the server, never raw audio. Opt-in only, behind a clear consent dialog.

Not chosen: "AI assistant" as a v1 differentiator. We don't have the data, and rushing ML before heuristics buys mystery without value.

Every suggestion carries a provenance tag (heuristic.resonance, ml.genre.pop, server.match) so we can a/b backends and the engineer can see why aux thinks what it thinks.

05 · Render & delivery

Browser-side, faster-than-realtime, batch-encoded. Server render only for outsized sessions or weak hardware.

The same DSP graph that runs at realtime in the AudioWorklet runs in a plain Web Worker offline at unbounded speed. Modern hardware delivers 20–40× realtime on a typical mix. A three-minute song masters in 5–10 seconds; the user already has the data and the compute, so we use it.

One offline render produces the master PCM buffer. Every delivery target is just a different encoding pass: WAV and FLAC via JS (cheap), MP3 and AAC via WebCodecs (also cheap). Batch all formats in parallel from the same buffer.

Server fallback triggers when: sessions exceed 100 tracks, or 50 plugins, or the user opens on hardware that fails a 5-second render benchmark on session load. The fallback is the same code, compiled for the server.

Not chosen: server-side rendering as the default (storage cost, upload latency, no point — the audio is already local). Wall-clock rendering (intolerably slow for batch delivery).

"Multi-format batch" in the export wireframe is cheap by construction, not by promise. The export queue is a list of encoders, not a list of renders.

06 · Storage

Two modes per session: Local or Cloud. Cloud enables share-link delivery; Local stays on-device. BYO bucket and real-time collab both land in v2.

The browser is aux's home, but the engineer's data doesn't have to be ours. Two modes, set per session — never per account, because some mixes are sensitive and others aren't:

Local. File System Access API picks a folder on disk; stems and session JSON live there. No upload, no remote access, no share-link. OPFS as the Safari/Firefox fallback — private app storage, persistent, not visible in Finder. This is the closest aux gets to feeling like a native desktop DAW, and the right choice for sensitive projects, unsigned artists, NDA work, and engineers who simply prefer their data on their disk.

Cloud. Stems direct to S3 (Cloudflare R2) via signed URLs from our API — the server never proxies audio. Multipart upload over 50 MB, resumable across reconnects. Hot tier for 30 days after last open, then R2 Infrequent Access with a brief restore indicator. Session document autosaves to the API; named snapshots are committed on demand. Default for new sessions.

Async sharing — Cloud mode only. Snapshot the session, generate a share link, send it. The recipient opens a read-only viewer of that exact mix state: play, scrub, switch reference rooms, view metering and pre-flight results — they cannot edit, save, or export. Think "send a PDF of a mix," not "join my live session." The link is a signed token referencing a specific snapshot; revoking it invalidates the token without touching the session.

BYO bucket (v2). Power users connect their own AWS S3, Cloudflare R2, or Backblaze B2. We hold encrypted credentials, never the audio. "Cloud" mode with someone else's invoice.

Real-time collab (v2+). When collab returns, the session document grows a Yjs CRDT layer over the same atom paths defined in §16.02. A Hybrid storage mode (stems local, session cloud, audio via WebRTC P2P) arrives at the same time. None of v1's storage code changes — the new mode is additive.

Not chosen: cloud-only (alienates privacy-first engineers, breaks for sensitive projects). Local-only with no share path (kills the producer-feedback loop the async link enables). Per-account mode locking (some mixes are sensitive, others aren't — the choice has to be per session).

Storage mode is the second thing the engineer sees in the create-session flow, after the name and before the stems drop. The choice shapes which UI surfaces light up: Local hides the share button entirely; Cloud shows it. No half-states. And: Local mode genuinely costs us nothing — which is what makes "free for everyone" (§18.05) economically sustainable.

07 · Browser support & perf envelope

Chrome / Edge 119+ full. Safari 17+ degraded. Firefox 121+ degraded. COOP+COEP required. Ship gate: 64 tracks × 4 plugins/channel + master at 48 kHz / 60 fps on a 2023-era mid-range Mac.

Chrome / Edge 119+: full support — SharedArrayBuffer, AudioWorklet, WebCodecs, WebGPU for spectrum/waveform rendering. This is the target.

Safari 17+: degraded — SAB requires specific headers; WebCodecs partial. Works for < 32 tracks. Banner at session open says "Safari is supported but Chrome is recommended for sessions over 32 tracks."

Firefox 121+: degraded — AudioWorklet performance ~30% slower on identical hardware. Same banner, lower threshold.

Pre-119 / IE / mobile browsers: hard block with a "this is a desktop tool" landing page. No half-broken experience.

18 · Decisions

Locked product decisions for v1

The six product calls that shape what the engineer sees. Pair these with §16 (how we build it) and §17 (where it stands).

01 · Plugins

Native-only at launch.

The native suite has to be deep enough that no engineer ever reaches for a third party. Bench for v1: a 4-band EQ with dynamic and linear-phase modes, two compressor characters (clean VCA + colored FET), de-esser, multiband comp, plate / hall / spring reverbs, tape and console saturation, transient designer, multiband limiter, M/S imager, exciter, tuner. If the suite can't stand next to FabFilter / Pulsar / SSL on quality, we delay the launch rather than ship something thin.

02 · Editing

Clip editing is in.

Stems aren't always perfect. The engineer needs to fade, trim, slip, crossfade, draw a clip-gain envelope, and silence a click at 2:14. No takes, no comping, no arrangement — but the basic clip toolbox is non-negotiable. Mute regions, ripple-less editing, snap-to-zero on the cut tool, and phase-rotate per clip are all in scope.

03 · Automation

Pro Tools grade. Phased.

Long-term target: lane-per-parameter automation with read / latch / touch / write modes — the full Pro Tools idiom. Phased delivery: v1 volume, pan, and send automation with read/write modes; v1.5 latch and touch; v2 plugin-parameter automation; v3 trim mode, curve shaping, snap-to-grid, automation consolidation. Every phase ships a working subset, never a half-working superset.

04 · Smart

Helpful, never automatic.

aux suggests; the engineer commits. Gain-staging hints, group-bus proposals on import, reference-matching as a visualization (here's where your spectrum differs from the reference), loudness-target nudges, clip gain suggestions on hot stems. We make every move worth considering visible — but we never push a fader on the engineer's behalf. The line is: assist the eyes and ears, never the hands.

05 · Pricing

Free. All of it.

No track limit. No export watermark. No paywalled plugins. No "Pro" tier for the people who actually do this for a living. The mixer is free, forever, for everyone. Monetization comes later from things adjacent to the mix — collaboration / storage / mastering services / studio business tools — but never by gating the engineer's instrument.

06 · Mobile

Desktop only.

No mixing on a phone. Ever. Mobile gets exactly one thing, eventually: a read-only listening view with A/B reference toggle, for the engineer who wants to A/B a master on their commute or check a render in the car. The session lives on the desktop; mobile is a window into it, not an editor.

19.3 · How we don't go bankrupt

"Free for everyone" (§18.05) is a principle, not a plan. The cost story works at 1,000 users (~$144/mo per implementation §13) and stays survivable at 10,000. Beyond that, we need a revenue thesis. Four tracks, in chronological order:

Track 1 · v1 · Free forever

The mixer, the suite, the surfaces — all of it

Everything in v1: the 12 native plugins, Local + Cloud storage, async share-links, Reference Rooms, Smart EQ, Pre-flight, Stem swap, all 8 wireframed surfaces. The mixer stays free. Period. Decision §18.05 binds this in writing.

Track 2 · v2+ · Pro tier · ~$15/mo

Things that genuinely cost us money per user

Real-time collab (Hocuspocus server + presence + WebRTC signaling) · Priority server-side render for slow hardware · Higher cloud storage caps (free: 50 GB / Pro: 500 GB) · Server-side ML smart engine (§16.04 v3) · Unlimited snapshot retention (free: last 90 days only). All have real per-user infra cost; charging for them aligns incentives.

Price target: ~$15/mo or $144/yr — Spotify-equivalent. Engineers happily pay this for tools they use daily.

Track 3 · v3+ · Marketplace · revenue share

Engineer-curated content as a platform

Named-engineer chains. "Andrew Scheps drum bus", "CLA vocal chain", "Manny Marroquin master bus" — presets sold at $5–20. 70/30 split with the engineer.

Third-party WASM plugins. Community devs publish via a registry under the same Plugin API contract as native (§16.03). Most plugins free; paid ones: 15–30% platform fee.

Mastering-as-a-service. Real mastering engineers using aux as their delivery platform. We take 10–15% per transaction. Distinct from the "auto-master" we explicitly will not build — humans master the mix.

Track 4 · v3+ · B2B · the long game

Studio business tools — where serious money lives

aux for Studios. Multi-seat plans for working studios: shared sessions across staff engineers, central client management, shared plugin libraries, invoicing, contract templates, client portals for project handoff. $50–200 / seat / month.

This is where engineers-as-businesses actually need software — and where legacy DAW vendors have never reached. The B2B layer is the long-term revenue base; the consumer mixer is the funnel into it.

Funding philosophy

We do not raise venture capital. VC math demands 10× returns in 5–7 years, which forces an exit or aggressive monetization that violates §18.05. Bootstrapping or revenue-funded growth lets us hold the free-for-everyone ethos longer. Stripe's model — make money from those who can pay, free for everyone else — is the closest analog. Worst case: if none of v2+ tiers find traction, the project survives on marketplace + mastering revenue. If even that fails, donations / grants / community funding exist. The mixer never gets gated.

21 · Accessibility

What aux can do that desktop DAWs can't

Desktop DAWs are structurally inaccessible. Pro Tools has had accessibility complaints for two decades. Web tech gives us a real chance to do better — not as compliance theatre, but because the same surfaces blind engineers and engineers in low-light bedrooms need are the surfaces fast keyboard engineers want. Accessibility done well is just better software.

The commitment

aux ships v1 at WCAG 2.2 AA, audited externally before launch. We treat it as a release gate, not a roadmap item.

What that means concretely

Keyboard

Every action reachable by key

No mouse-only paths. Tab order is logical and visible (focus-visible everywhere). Every shortcut from §15 works without modifiers conflicting with the browser. Command palette (/) covers what shortcuts don't.

Screen reader

Meters and metering announce

Every knob, fader, and meter has an ARIA label with live value updates throttled to 250 ms (no rapid-fire announcements). LUFS readouts announce on focus and on threshold cross. Channel selection announces channel name + current insert chain.

Contrast

4.5:1 minimum, high-contrast theme

All text + meaningful UI passes 4.5:1 in default theme. A built-in high-contrast theme pushes everything to 7:1. Color is never the only signal — every status (pass/warn/fail) carries an icon and a label too.

Motion

Reduced motion respected everywhere

prefers-reduced-motion disables knob rotation animations, fader smoothing visualizations, spectrum decay rendering, and Reference Room transitions. The audio doesn't change; the visual flair does.

The hard problem · the EQ canvas

A graphical EQ is inherently visual. Two answers:

  • Numeric mode. Tab into the EQ canvas; an overlay appears with each band as a labeled row (freq · gain · Q · type). Edit by typing or arrow keys. Screen readers announce each band on focus.
  • Sonified curve. Optional mode: as the engineer scrubs band by band, aux plays a tone-sweep modulated by the current curve at that band's frequency — hear the shape, not just the numbers.
Side benefit

The keyboard-first commitment makes aux faster for sighted engineers too. Mix engineers already live on the keyboard; we just hold ourselves to the standard that says every move is accessible from there. The accessibility audit is also the fastest-mix audit.

22 · Security

Threat model

The cloud surface and the share-link viewer are the attack surface. Local mode (§16.06) genuinely has none — stems never leave the engineer's machine. For everything else, here's the threat model, one row per vector. Each gets a mitigation; nothing relies on user vigilance.

VectorRiskMitigation
Signed-URL minting SSRF, unauthorized access to another user's stems Server validates user.id owns session.id before signing; URL scoped to one object; 5-min TTL; no redirects honored
Share-link tokens Replay, scope creep, can't revoke once sent JWT with snapshot_id + share_id + exp; share_id is a DB row; revoke = row delete; token scoped to one snapshot only, no edit grants
Stem upload Malformed audio crashes the worklet; polyglot files (WAV that's secretly HTML) Server-side header parse (magic-number check), extension match, size cap; decoded once in a sandboxed Worker before reaching the audio thread
Session metadata XSS Track names, session names, preset names rendered into HTML Sanitize on display (DOMPurify); strict CSP rejects inline scripts; name fields are text-content-only on render, never innerHTML
Account takeover Magic-link interception, reuse, link-sharing One-time use; 10-min expiry; new device requires fresh auth; rate-limited per email; suspicious-IP warnings via email
API abuse Spamming render queue, upload endpoint, share-link mint Token-bucket rate limit per user (Redis); stricter IP-based limit on unauthenticated endpoints (signup, magic-link request)
WASM supply chain Malicious plugin update, compromised dependency v1: all native plugins built in CI, hash-pinned, Subresource Integrity on script tags. v2 third-party plugins go through code review + sandboxed-execution validation before listing
Share-link enumeration Guessing token URLs to find others' shared mixes Tokens are 256-bit signed JWTs — not enumerable; revoked tokens fail validation; share_id rotates if engineer regenerates
Incident response

Two practiced flows before launch: (a) revoke compromised auth — single SQL drops all sessions for a user, force re-auth on next request; (b) revoke share links en masse — single SQL invalidates all share_id rows for a session. Both rehearsed quarterly. No security incident waits on figuring out how to respond.

23 · Presets

Plugin presets as a first-class concept

Engineers live by presets. "My vocal chain", "Andrew Scheps drum bus", "the kick EQ I always start from." If presets aren't first-class, they become folder-on-Dropbox-of-XML-files (Pro Tools) or buried inside individual plugin UIs (Studio One). aux makes them an explicit part of the session model and the user account.

Three types

01 · Built-in

Ship with the plugin

Curated by us. Read-only. Example: EQ-8 ships with "Voice / Female", "Voice / Male", "Drum bus low-cut", "Master bus tilt". Loaded from a frozen JSON inside the plugin's WASM package.

02 · User presets

Saved per account · follow the engineer

"My vocal EQ", "My drum bus comp." Stored against the user, not the session — available in any new session. Save via the plugin-header dropdown.

03 · Chain presets

Multi-plugin templates

"My vocal chain" = EQ-8 + DeEss + Comp-A + Verb-Send, with each plugin's state. Saved at the channel level; restorable to any channel in any session.

In the schema

UI · where the engineer finds them

  • Plugin header dropdown — every plugin window has a preset menu (Save as · Load · Manage). One-click apply.
  • Channel header dropdown — chain presets live here. "Apply 'My vocal chain'" replaces the entire insert chain on that strip.
  • Command palette — /preset vocal fuzzy-finds across all preset types.

Migration when a plugin schema changes

Each plugin has a schema_version. Presets carry the version they were saved under. When EQ-8 v2 ships with new parameters, loading a v1 preset runs the plugin's migrate(v1_state) function — defined alongside the DSP per §16.03. No engineer ever loses their chain to a plugin upgrade.

v3 marketplace path

The preset schema is the foundation for the marketplace tier (§19.3 Track 3). Named-engineer chains are just chain-presets with an attribution field and a price. Same data model, additive UI. No re-architecture needed.

24 · Engineer-in-residence

Closing the feedback loop

§12 named the engineer-in-residence role. This section defines how the role actually changes the product. Without a documented loop, the role becomes ceremonial — a logo on the about page, not a force on the backlog.

The weekly cadence

  • Monday — Tuesday. EIR uses aux for their real, paying mix work. At least 4 sessions, >1 hour each. Real client deadlines, real stakes. Records audio commentary with the in-app "Engineer cam" (consent-gated, stored against the EIR's account).
  • Wednesday — 30-min sync. 5 min what worked · 15 min painpoints (the list from the week, ranked) · 10 min team votes on the top 3 to bring into the next sprint.
  • Friday. The voted-in painpoints are tickets in the next sprint with the EIR's session recording linked as the reproduction case.

Practices

  • Session recording with consent. The EIR opts into "Engineer cam" — audio narration while they work, timestamped to the session timeline. Future: this becomes a product feature (§14 "Voice-narrated mix history" idea) but starts as an internal tool.
  • Five-whys on every painpoint. Keep asking until the root cause is clear. "The EQ canvas felt slow" → why? → "the spectrum redraws on every drag" → why? → "no debouncing on the band-move handler" → fix is one line, not a rewrite.
  • Time-boxing the no. No painpoint sits longer than 2 sprints without an explicit decision: shipped, deferred to v2+, or refused (no-list). No painpoints rot in the backlog.

The role's KPIs

Lifted from §19.2: 4+ real sessions per week, no complaints. All painpoints triaged within one week. Zero painpoints waiting past two sprints without an explicit decision. The EIR's complaints are the ground-truth product backlog.

Why this works

A working mix engineer using the product weekly under real deadlines surfaces friction no internal team can fake. The product team is too close to the code; users in surveys are too polite. The EIR is the one person allowed to be brutally honest because we asked them to be. Treat their bug list as the most credible artifact in the company.
